\subsection{Background}
Coastal and brackish water lagoons that form continuities in the terrestrial and shelf ecosystems present the transitional zones between land and sea. They are often associated with dynamic environmental conditions and biodiversity due to their connections to the marine and terrestrial communities (XXX). Formed as a result of the marine transgression, coastal lagoons vary with depth, salinity, geomorphic features (slope and sediment substrate types) and may include diverse tidal regime (XXX; XXX). The complex processes of the formation of the barriers between the land and ocean are undergone modifications by controversial forces of erosion and sediment deposition within the coastal lagoons. The intensity of accumulation of river sediments depends on the river speed which transport sediments in large quantity, and intensified through the hydrological  processes  such as winds, oceanic currents and coastal waves. 

There is little systematic research analysing and contrasting coastal lagoons worldwide. Only a few studies exist to draw generalisations regarding the deep links between the ecosystems of coastal lagoons and the adjacent habitat communities (XXX). They are however biodiversity hotspots for both terrestrial (birds) and marine populations (fish species) (XXX). Topographically isolated and hydrologically distinct from the surrounding landscapes, coastal lagoons include unique species of wild flora and fauna (e.g., bird species). Due to high levels of biodiversity, coastal lagoons are considered as natural heritage and classified as areas of wildlife conservation (XXX). Due to the rich bioproductivity and valuable environment, coastal lagoons are declared as marine protected areas worldwide (XXX). At the same time, the transitional nature of coastal lagoons makes them vulnerable to the cumulative effects from the climate fluctuations, hydrological disturbances and human impacts (XXX, XXX). Such high level of the stress results in the environmental threat to these treasured ecosystems.

\section{Study Area}
The coastal lagoon formed by the Chilika Lake lies within the Odisha state of India, at the mouth of the Daya River, entering the Bay of Bengal on the east coast of India (Figure \ref{fig01}). The largest brackish water lagoon in Asia, Chilika Lake coveres a total area of over 1100 km2 (XXX). The Chilika Lake is located at the junction of two different water masses - riverine freshwater and oceanic salt waters from the tidal influx of the Bay of Bengal, and these interact with the bathymetry of the lagoon to generate local hydrographic setting such as varying salinity, current turbulence and circular flow patterns of waves (XXX). A recent geological study has shown that Chilika Lake was part of the Bay of Bengal during the later stages of the Pleistocene period (XXX), and there is archaeological evidence for human settlements existing in the Chilika Lake area and used is as marine harbour and port since at least Neolithic period (XXX). The occupation of the Chilika Lake is explained by the favourable climate setting and its beneficial location which gave access to the Indian Ocean and ensured maritime trade and international commercial connections in ancient India. The importance of the Chilika Lake in both ecological aspect and for historical value for the development of Indian civilisation resulted in its proposal as UNESCO World Heritage site (XXX).
